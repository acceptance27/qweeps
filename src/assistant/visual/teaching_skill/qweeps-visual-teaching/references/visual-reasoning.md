# Visual reasoning for infographics

The observed failure: the first WWI example had moving labels, colored tracks,
and a decision circle, but most of the explanation remained in the narration.
The user accepted Python, ALU, and world generation, where an observable state
changes. Do not solve an infographic failure by adding more facts or arrows.

## Choose what the viewer needs to see

Start from the difficult relationship, not a list of section headings. Write a
`visual_design`: the relationship family, why the chosen form exposes it, what
position/size/color/line style mean, the likely misreading, and its on-screen
safeguard. Do not use measured-looking geometry for unmeasured claims.

| Relationship or difficulty | Useful visual operation | What must become apparent |
|---|---|---|
| Difference between approaches | Align the same concrete case on common dimensions; change one treatment | Exactly what differs and what stays the same |
| Category membership or overlap | Sort recognizable cases into regions; move a boundary or a borderline case | The classification rule, including ambiguous cases |
| Feedback | Follow one state through action, measured outcome, and a changed next decision | Why the next cycle differs; returning arrows alone show little |
| Parts, hierarchy, abstraction | Build from a concrete instance; regroup the same objects into a larger unit | What each abstract region contains, without losing identity |
| Quantity or proportion | Count units, group them, then transform them into an area/bar on a stated scale | Which whole is being divided and what size means |
| Evidence and decisions | Enact a concrete consequence, then transform it into a retained evidence thumbnail beside the decision | Why it matters to that actor, and what it cannot establish |
| Time and causation | Use time for ordering; separately show the mechanism/evidence linking conditions to choices | Earlier is not automatically causal; chronology and explanation have different jobs |
| Misconception | Show the prediction the proposed model makes; compare it with an observation | Why the model fails, rather than merely crossing out its name |

The PDF reference supplies a useful visual vocabulary, not a guarantee of clarity.
A tour of unrelated diagram types can still lose the learner. Carry one concrete
case across views and explain why the next view answers a question the last one
left open. A quadrant needs meaningful axes and placed examples; a curve needs
defined quantities and a basis, or an explicit illustrative status.

A symbolic derivation may teach through changing code or equations themselves;
keep the corresponding terms identifiable and earn each transformation. Do not
add gratuitous icons to satisfy a geometry check. This exception is for symbolic
structure, not for prose labels in an infographic.

A shape is useful when its spatial relation encodes something. Boxes can be valid
states in a comparison; icons can identify entities. Neither becomes evidence just
because it moves. Choose several forms within a lesson only when the relationship
changes, as in PDF Explainer. Keep a small overview or a recognizable anchor when
entering a detail. Camera motion is navigation, not the argument itself.

## Design each transformation

1. State the learner's unresolved question.
2. Give a concrete visual referent: this ship route, this program variable, this
   choice, this sample. Establish its meaning before abbreviating it.
3. Change a visible relationship: interrupt the route, regroup the cases, align
   alternatives, change the boundary, expose the shared subset. Preserve enough
   of the prior state to support the comparison.
4. Make a specific inference possible. Record what remains uncertain.

Use a short label to identify the relation (e.g. “proposed, conditional alliance”).
Do not expect the viewer to infer a coded line style without introducing it. In
history, a dashed proposal must not become a solid accomplished alliance. Arrows
can mean movement, sequence, influence, membership, or a proposal: choose and
label the role. Do not animate causal certainty as a signal flowing through a
circuit unless the subject really has that mechanism.

## Review the visual argument

**With narration and long captions removed**, can a viewer identify the entities,
notice the important difference, and answer the beat's question from the geometry
plus short labels? Short labels are allowed; a paragraph restating the answer is
not a visual argument. Record a concrete observation and inference, not “clear”.

Then review the complete infographic, including its descriptions. This diagnostic
does not forbid explanatory text: dates, definitions, actor identities and short
accounts of what happened are often essential. The user preferred the supplied
HyperFrames Rizal reference precisely because its descriptions made unfamiliar
history followable. Do not optimize for fewer words at the cost of comprehension.

Then test the most plausible wrong reading: did equal areas imply equal causal
weight? Did a map imply an accurate geography? Did a telegram proposal look like
an accepted pact? Did a color change look like a new entity? State the safeguard
that is actually visible. Do not declare that a model-based semantic review proves
learning; the artifact still needs frame and playback inspection.

**Attention and timing:** introduce one unfamiliar relation at a time; let it
register, change it during the relevant spoken clause, then pause at the outcome.
A single narration clip starting over the previous scene can explain an object
before it appears. Use per-clause cue times when necessary. A fixed total beat
length or several seconds of silence at the end cannot repair that mismatch.

## Worked repair: US entry into WWI

Weak: “shipping” dots flow toward “weigh response”; then “security” dots do the
same. The moving dots have no historical referent and the learner supplies why.

Better: trace an illustrative neutral trade route, compare warning restrictions
with unrestricted attacks, and visibly interrupt that same route. Keep a small
route image when changing to the diplomatic relationship. Show Germany proposing
a conditional pact to Mexico, a dashed proposal rather than an accepted alliance,
and put the US in the relationship so the security implication is visible. Keep
both concrete thumbnails beside the later institutional request/declaration.
Show that neutrality persisted between the telegram and war request, so the diagram
does not imply a message automatically caused a declaration. No invented weights,
probabilities, or alternate-history outcomes are needed.
