# Teaching method

The central unit is an explanatory transformation, not a slide, frame, loop
iteration, drawing command, or arbitrary time interval. The useful question for a
beat is: **What can the learner now infer from what visibly changed?**

## Choose the progression from the concept

Start with the learner's question and one concrete example they can track. Decide
what they already understand and the specific understanding they should gain.
Then pick a representation that exposes the relationship: a spatial comparison,
a geometric construction, a mechanism with state, a causal timeline, a graph
and changing quantity, or an evolving worked example. A box saying "innovation"
explains little by itself; a changed relationship between resources, action and
outcome may show the mechanism. A moving dot is useful when it represents a
specific signal, value, candidate, person, or event.

A common progression is problem → insufficient baseline → useful construction →
visible consequence → generalization or experiment. This is a choice, not a
mandatory five-beat template. The PDF reference organizes related conceptual
regions on a spatial blackboard; Math 01 follows a learning mechanism through
several representations; A* earns a heuristic formula through visible distances.
Use the progression that fits the question.

## Build understanding through the same example

Introduce a small number of meaningful objects before their symbols. Assign
stable roles and colours. Construct connections and quantities as the narration
makes them relevant. Preserve objects through a comparison so the learner can
attribute the outcome to the changed condition. Geometry should substantiate an
equation, numerical readout, or causal statement rather than decorate it.

For a meaningful transition, specify:

- The question left open by the prior scene.
- What changes in the model or representation.
- What the learner should notice because of that change.
- The conclusion this makes possible, including its assumptions.

Do not merely rename a heading, highlight the next item, or recite different
coordinates with the same sentence. Keep repeated execution running within the
same explanatory beat. Pause or create another beat when a qualitatively new
idea appears, such as a tie, detour, failed assumption, feedback effect, or change
of metric.

## Camera, narration, and timing serve the inference

Use the camera to answer "where should I look now?" Keep the surrounding model
available for orientation. Spatial blackboards may reveal another region while
leaving earlier regions in the same world. Do not clear the entire canvas by
habit. A static camera is appropriate when it makes a comparison clearer.

First let a new object register, then explain its role. Trace paths and change
quantities while discussing the relationship. Leave a short pause after the
important reveal. A 1.5-second first-reveal pause is the project's current
convention, not a guarantee of good timing. Narration can extend the end of a
beat; it must not freeze its construction midway through. Captions must still
teach when sound is off.

Narration explains why a visible relationship matters. It should not recite
"Create a circle" or "pan the camera." The animation and narration should have
complementary jobs. Each teaching beat advances the learner's mental model.

## End in a usable model

Return to the opening question and make the answer visible. Where useful, vary
one input or rule and let the learner predict the outcome. For source-based
explanations, attach conclusions to actual code or evidence. For historical or
social explanations, distinguish causal evidence, comparison and analogy; do
not turn an aesthetic geometric metaphor into a factual claim.
